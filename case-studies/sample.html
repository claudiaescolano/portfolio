Case study · 2025

Reimagining the analytics dashboard.

How I rebuilt a fintech analytics surface from the ground up — and landed a 47% increase in feature adoption.

RoleLead Product Designer
Duration4 months
Team1 PM, 3 engineers, me
ToolsFigma, Dovetail, Maze
The problem

Users couldn't find what they needed.

The legacy dashboard had grown organically over four years. Eighteen tabs, three navigation patterns, and a feature graveyard. Engagement metrics showed users were bouncing within 12 seconds of landing — barely enough time to scan the page.

Customer support tickets told a clear story: people knew the data they needed existed somewhere in the product. They just couldn't find it.

"I know it's in here. I just don't have time to dig for it." — Customer interview, March 2025
Process

Research first, design second.

I started with eight customer interviews and a card-sorting study with 24 power users. The pattern was unambiguous: users mentally grouped features around four jobs — monitor, investigate, report, share — but the navigation reflected our internal team structure, not theirs.

From there I sketched five layout directions in low fidelity and tested two with users via Maze. The "command bar + workspace" pattern came out clearly ahead, with task completion times 38% faster than the runner-up.

Key decisions

What we cut, and why.

The hardest part wasn't designing the new interface — it was choosing what to leave out. Three legacy features had vocal but small user bases. After looking at usage data and revenue impact, we deprecated two and folded one into a new "advanced" mode.

One direction I tried and abandoned: an AI-summary panel at the top of the dashboard. Early prototypes felt magical, but testing revealed users didn't trust auto-generated insights for decisions that affected their financials. We shelved it for a future release with stronger explainability.

Solution

A workspace, not a website.

The redesigned dashboard is organized around a persistent command bar that lets users pivot between any of the four jobs without losing context. We built a saved-views system so power users can return to their personalized setups instantly.

Visually, we leaned into hierarchy and breathing room — fewer borders, more typography, generous whitespace. Data became the thing the eye lands on first.

What shipped, and what it changed.

Critical regressions at rollout

The launch went out to 100% of users without a phased rollback. Two months in, retention on the analytics surface is up 23% and support tickets about navigation have dropped to near-zero.

Lessons

What I'd do differently.

I'd run the AI-summary test earlier. We spent two weeks building polished prototypes before learning users didn't trust the underlying logic — we could have caught that with a paper test in two days.
