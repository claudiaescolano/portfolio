§ Context
01Context

The client — a national educators' association with a large member base — used Arrivia for some of their travel benefits and a competitor for others. The competitor saw an opening and made a strategic move: they offered to build the client a unified Hub, a single destination consolidating all member benefits — travel, shopping, dining, the works — under their roof. The pitch was clear. Move everything to us, and your members get one experience instead of three.

The client was seriously considering it. If they accepted, Arrivia would lose the relationship entirely.

I was asked to design the counter-pitch. The brief was direct: prove that Arrivia could deliver the same unified experience, but better, with the existing travel breadth they already had with us. One week to design and prototype the entire thing.

§ Approach
02Approach

A pitch defense is a different design problem than a product redesign. The work isn't optimizing for daily use — it's optimizing for belief. The client had to walk out of a meeting convinced Arrivia could ship the future the competitor was only describing.

Lead with the experience, not the architecture.

The competitor's pitch was a description of a hub. My counter had to be a visceral demonstration of one. That meant a high-fidelity prototype the client could see and feel, and a video walking through the full member journey end to end — not a deck describing what we would build. The medium was the message: if our pitch was more polished and more concrete than the competitor's, the client was seeing the future Arrivia could deliver, not imagining it.

Design for the breadth Arrivia already had.

The competitor's hub was vaporware — a promise. Arrivia already had hotel, cruise, air, resort, rental car, tours, and shopping/dining benefits live and shipping. The hub design needed to make that breadth feel like an advantage, not a feature list. I designed the hub around member journeys — planning a trip, redeeming benefits, discovering perks — rather than around product categories, which is what made the breadth feel cohesive instead of cluttered.

Build the future and the present in the same pitch.

The hub had to show what existed today and what the next phase could look like. That dual framing matters in pitch design — current capabilities prove credibility, future-state design proves vision. I designed both, clearly differentiated, in a single prototype flow.

Make the prototype video the centerpiece.

Most pitches lead with a deck and reference designs. I inverted it. The video — a walkthrough of a member's full travel journey across hotel, cruise, air, and benefits — was the artifact the meeting was built around. Designs and discussion supported the video, not the other way around.

§ The Work
03The Work

The pitch deliverable was a high-fidelity Hub prototype and the walkthrough video — the full member experience on a single page. Landing on the Hub, browsing travel and non-travel benefits side-by-side, planning a multi-product trip, redeeming rewards — all in one place. The video tied it all together as a coherent journey.

The prototype was designed to feel inevitable — like the version of Arrivia the client should have been getting all along. That was the strategic frame: not "here's a new direction," but "here's what we already are, made visible."

Post-Pitch · What Shipped

The pitch won the meeting. What shipped to NEA members is a refined version of the Hub — adjusted through stakeholder iteration and scoped to what the engineering team could deliver in the first release. The architectural decisions held; the visual direction evolved.

§ Impact
04Impact

The client stayed.

After the counter-pitch, the client decided not to move forward with the competitor's hub offer and renewed their commitment to Arrivia. The design work was a direct factor in that decision — the prototype gave the client a concrete reason to believe Arrivia could deliver the unified experience the competitor was only promising.

The deliverable also became a reference asset internally. The hub design and prototype video continued to be used in conversations about how Arrivia could position its full product breadth to other partners — meaning a one-week pitch defense ended up shaping how the company tells its story.

§ Reflection
05Reflection

The lesson from this project: in a pitch defense, the design has to do the convincing, because nobody trusts the words.

The competitor's offer was words — a hub they promised to build. Arrivia's counter could have been words too — a deck describing what we'd build in response. That would have been a fair fight, and we likely would have lost it, because the competitor had already framed the conversation. The decision to lead with a working prototype and a polished video shifted the terms entirely. Words competing with words is a coin flip. A working prototype competing with words is a different category.

This is the project that taught me the most about design as a strategic medium, not just a craft. When the stakes are real and the timeline is short, the question isn't what's the best design — it's what's the artifact that wins this meeting? Sometimes those answers are the same. Sometimes they're not. Knowing the difference is part of the senior job.

What I'd do differently: I'd want to follow the design through the renewed engagement and see how much of the pitched hub actually shipped, in what shape, and how members responded to it. I owned the design that won the meeting. Owning what happens after is the next level of the work.
