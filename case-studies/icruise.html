§ Context
01Context

iCruise is the odd product in Arrivia's portfolio. Most of Arrivia's business is B2B — travel loyalty programs powering hotels, rental cars, flights, tours, and cruises for partner brands behind closed user groups (CUG). iCruise is the exception: a public-facing, no-login-required, retail cruise booking site that anyone can visit.

When I joined the project, the existing iCruise was a 90s-era interface — visually dated, structurally confusing, dense with information but nearly impossible to digest. It was actively losing potential customers to cleaner, faster competitors.

The original brief was deceptively simple: rebuild the entire flow — homepage, search, results, booking funnel, checkout — for both the public-facing version and the CUG version (where members see their rewards balances, cruise cash, and partner-branded experience). One specific constraint: cut the number of clicks needed to complete a booking, end-to-end. The strategic goal was conversion: turn anonymous visitors into rewards-program members by making the savings and benefits of joining feel undeniable in the flow itself.

I co-led the redesign end-to-end with another senior designer, equal scope. We shared system thinking and design ownership across the full experience. I individually owned the interactive prototype work that ended up driving the partner pitches.

§ Approach
02Approach

The redesign and the partner pitches were technically two outputs, but the design thinking was unified. A few decisions shaped both:

Design for two audiences in one system.

The public version had to convert. The CUG version had to retain. Same product, two completely different jobs-to-be-done. The design system had to flex enough to surface rewards and savings prominently for logged-in members, while making the value of becoming a member obvious to anonymous users. We designed both versions in parallel, not sequentially — every component had to work in both contexts from day one.

Build for white-label from the start.

Arrivia's broader business is partner-branded. Even though iCruise itself is the company's own retail site, we knew the design system would eventually be re-skinned for CUG partners. We made that architectural choice early — color tokens, branding surfaces, currency labels, and rewards terminology were all parameterized. This decision is what made the airline and telecom wins possible later. They weren't pivots. They were the system working as designed.

Treat the prototype as a sales artifact, not a design artifact.

Most prototypes are built for stakeholder review. I built these to be presented to prospective partners — interactive end-to-end flows that could be re-skinned and walked through in a 20-minute pitch meeting. That distinction shaped everything: which flows were prioritized, how polished the empty states had to be, how the demo was paced.

Compete with the named competitor directly.

The competitor was named on multiple deals and the reason iCruise was being passed over. We built the redesign with their product open in a second tab — not to copy, but to understand the bar and identify where we could leap past it. The wins came from the places we didn't match them.

§ The Work
03The Work

The redesign covered the full booking journey: homepage, search, results, ship and itinerary details, cabin selection, booking flow, and confirmation. Every page was rebuilt against a system that flexed between public-facing and CUG modes — surfacing rewards and member savings for logged-in users while making the value of joining feel concrete to anonymous ones.

Mid-Project · The Airline Customization

While we were finishing the redesign, a major US airline came into the picture as a CUG partner pitch. Their members would only ever see the logged-in version, so the prototype I built for the pitch was the CUG flow re-skinned: airline branding, the airline's rewards currency, their terminology throughout. This was the first real-world test of the white-label architecture. It worked.

Mid-ProjectAlaska Airlines-branded prototype above the iCruise default — the white-label architecture's first real-world test.

Post-Pitch · The T-Mobile Switch

T-Mobile became an Arrivia client during the redesign, but only for the hotel and rental car products. They were using a competitor for their cruise business — the old iCruise hadn't been approved by their team, which was a fair read of the old product. After the redesign was complete, we customized a high-fidelity prototype to the T-Mobile brand and pitched the new iCruise as a competitor replacement.

They moved their cruise business to Arrivia on the strength of that prototype — before any of the new product was built. The contract was signed against design.

T-Mobile Travel, powered by arrivia, launched in November 2025 in phases — the homepage and select itinerary pages first, with the rest of the booking flow rolling out across subsequent phases. By Q1 2026, even with only the front of the funnel running on the new design, performance was tracking 202% → 347% above the prior competitor's historical booking volume, and revenue 176% → 319% above the same baseline — roughly 3x the volume the previous provider had been doing.

That's phase-one performance, not the full product. The rest is still rolling out.

§ Impact
04Impact

Three outcomes, one design effort.

The public-facing redesign shipped, replacing a decade-plus-old interface with a product that could credibly compete in 2025. The conversion path from anonymous visitor to CUG member was rebuilt around making rewards and savings feel concrete in-flow rather than buried in marketing pages.

A major US airline became a customization partner mid-project, validating the white-label architecture before it had even fully launched.

T-Mobile moved their cruise business to Arrivia after the prototype pitch, displacing a direct competitor — on the strength of design alone, before any of the new product had been built. T-Mobile Travel, powered by arrivia, launched in phases starting November 2025. Through Q1 2026, even with only the homepage and select itinerary pages running on the new design, the program was already tracking roughly 3x the prior provider's booking volume. That outcome is the clearest dollar-impact line on the project — and the redesign was the direct reason for it.

Internally, the project changed how the design team thought about prototyping. Prototypes had been treated as design deliverables. After iCruise, they were treated as business deliverables — equipment for the sales team, not just for the engineering handoff.

§ Reflection
05Reflection

The most useful lesson from this project wasn't about cruise booking or about prototypes. It was about systems that compound.

The white-label architecture wasn't on the original brief. We added it because it was the right design call — a flexible token system is just better systems thinking than a hardcoded one. But that decision is what produced the airline win, the telecom win, and every future partner conversation that uses the same prototype machinery. One quiet architectural decision early in the project paid off three different ways across a year.

That's the senior-design lesson I keep coming back to: the work that matters most is rarely on the brief. It's the foundational decisions you make when nobody's measuring whether you make them, that turn out to define what's possible later.

What I'd do differently: post-launch analytics on the public-facing conversion goal. The partner-side wins are clean. The B2C metrics — public-to-member conversion lift, search-to-checkout funnel, return visitor behavior — would have made the case study airtight. Owning the measurement loop, not just the design loop, is the habit I'm most actively building this year.
